HOW TO THINK THE SCRIPT THROUGH

Before writing any line, work the episode all the way through:

1. What is the one idea the viewer should understand? Write it as one sentence. The whole episode is that sentence, not three topics.
2. What mistaken belief causes [CHARACTER]'s first problem, and what concrete world belongs to this topic? Use [TOPIC_WORLD] as needed.
3. What two escalating mistakes will they make while acting on that belief?
4. What does each consequence help them notice? After the second mistake, what simple correct approach can they test so the viewer sees the solution work?
5. What is the final usable rule, stated naturally for the viewer after [CHARACTER] understands it?
6. After the spoken lines exist, mark Custom on every line. Draw reactions and lesson clicks as scenes too ([CHARACTER]'s face and body in a concrete moment), not as expression-library faces.
7. Never leave a spoken line without Custom.
8. How will this land at about 100 spoken lines—roughly 90–110—and about 500–700 words, without padding or fragmenting one thought across unnecessary lines?
9. Keyword pass: which 3–5 search phrases from the topic/title belong in spoken narration (not a laundry list)? Name the primary phrase (usually already in the title) and two secondaries that appear in the lesson.

Do not start writing until you can name the one idea, the two escalating mistakes, the correct attempt, and the moment [CHARACTER] finally understands.

Then write. Invent the voice line and its picture together. Custom is the only picture mode.

FORMAT

Every spoken line uses this format:

Custom: Voice line | Unspoken shot note

Example:

Custom: [CHARACTER] tried the obvious fix first. | Full body, [CHARACTER] attempts the first mistaken plan with confidence.
Custom: To them, that felt like a clever shortcut. | Full body, [CHARACTER] grins beside the setup that will fail.

The words after | are the shot note. They are not spoken and they are not a second line. Every Custom line needs one. Say what the viewer must see, whether [CHARACTER] is full body, and name the detail. If the line might run near 5 seconds, end the note with "Crop the feet." or "Crop the knees." That crop is the same file, not a new generation.

CHARACTER AND VOICE

The character is always [CHARACTER]. One consistent narrator tells the entire story about them.

The narrator:

- Speaks in natural, conversational English with complete thoughts and clear cause-and-effect.
- Refers to [CHARACTER] by name when establishing a beat, then naturally uses pronouns. Do not begin every line with the name.
- Uses past tense for the story and present tense for durable lessons.
- Can address the viewer as "you" when connecting the discovery to a practical takeaway.
- Does not impersonate [CHARACTER], speak as "I" on their behalf, or switch between narrator and teacher voices.
- Does not use caveman grammar, sentence fragments for style, or clipped keyword lines.
- Sounds warm, observant, lightly amused, and easy to follow—not academic, childish, or like a generic YouTuber intro.

Story lines (the problem, the plan, and the consequence):

- Narrate what [CHARACTER] believes and why the mistaken choice makes sense to them.
- Let humor come from confidence and the visible result, not broken grammar.
- Use transitions so each action follows naturally: "At first," "So," "But," "The next day," "That was when," and "This time."
- Keep each line concise enough for one breath, but do not split a normal sentence into choppy fragments merely to create more scenes.

Discovery lines:

- Slow slightly and connect the evidence to the lesson.
- Name and explain the concept only after the story has made it necessary.
- End with [CHARACTER] testing the better approach, seeing the result, and leaving the viewer with one memorable rule.

Do not add redundant narration, full-episode recaps, or "in this video" framing.

EPISODE SHAPE

Target about 100 spoken lines. Let the recorded narration determine the finished runtime.

Write about 90–110 spoken lines, aiming near 100, and roughly 500–700 spoken words. Most lines should be one complete conversational thought, commonly 5–10 words. Every newline is still one scene and one recording take.

One lesson per episode. Tell one connected story with two escalating mistakes followed by one correct attempt and payoff. Do not teach three different topics. Do not pad with recaps or “in this video” framing.

Opening, once: Promise, Proof, Plan in the narrator's conversational voice. Confirm what the viewer clicked for, begin inside [CHARACTER]'s problem, and signal that the story will reveal what actually works. Do not give away the entire conclusion before the story earns it.

If TOPIC includes a thumbnail brief formatted as Emotion, Visual, Text, and Open loop, the first 5–8 lines must immediately show that situation or consequence. The episode must answer the stated open loop.

Each mistake cycle, in this order:

1. Issue. Establish the concrete problem. Custom-heavy.
2. Mistaken reasoning. Explain why the plan seems to work.
3. Foolish fix. Act with confidence.
4. Consequence. Visible proof. Custom-heavy.
5. Partial discovery. Connect that proof to one new piece of the same lesson without stopping for a lecture.

Default story:

- Mistake 1 exposes the basic problem.
- Mistake 2 is more costly, awkward, risky, or absurd and deepens the same lesson.
- Correct attempt: [CHARACTER] uses what the consequences taught them, tries the simple better approach, and sees it work.
- Payoff: the narrator connects the result to one memorable rule the viewer can use.
- A short callback to the opening is welcome. Then stop. Do not write a like, subscribe, comment, or next-video closer; the app appends a fixed Custom channel outro.

If the draft is under about 90 lines, strengthen setup, causal transitions, visible consequences, callback, or successful test before adding another mistake. If it is over about 110 lines, combine fragmented thoughts and cut repeated explanation.
